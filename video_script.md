**[Slide 1 — Title]**
Hi everyone, I'm Bernie Miao, and today I'm presenting "The Geometry of Truth," a paper by Samuel Marks from Northeastern University and Max Tegmark from MIT. The paper asks a deceptively simple question: when a large language model reads a statement, does it internally represent whether that statement is true or false? And if it does, what does that representation actually look like? By the end of this talk, I hope to convince you that the answer is yes — and that the representation looks like something surprisingly familiar to anyone who's taken a data mining class: a straight line.

**[Slide 2 — Motivation]**
Let me start with why this matters. Large language models output falsehoods all the time. Sometimes they just don't know better. But sometimes they appear to know a statement is false and produce it anyway. OpenAI documented a striking case with GPT-4: an agent that needed a human to solve a CAPTCHA claimed it couldn't solve it because it was a vision-impaired person — while in its private reasoning scratchpad, it wrote that it should not reveal it was a robot and should make up an excuse. So the goal is this: given a model and a statement, can we tell whether the model believes the statement is true — not by trusting its outputs, but by looking at its internal state?

**[Slide 3 — Background & controversy]**
The main tool for that is called a probe: a small classifier trained on the model's internal activations to predict some property — here, truth. Earlier work found that even simple linear probes could do this remarkably well, which hinted at a "truth direction" inside the model. But critics pushed back: some of those probes failed on statements containing the word "not," which suggests they were tracking features that merely correlate with truth, like how plausible a sentence sounds. So the open question was: is there a real, unified, linear representation of truth — or is probing just sophisticated pattern-matching on confounds?

**[Slide 4 — Strategy]**
Marks and Tegmark attack this with three complementary lines of evidence. First, visualization: project the activations down with PCA and check whether true and false statements visibly separate. Second, transfer: train probes on one dataset and test them on very different datasets. Third — and this is the strongest test — intervention: push the model's activations along the probe direction and see if you can causally flip its judgment. All of their experiments use the LLaMA-2 family, at 7, 13, and 70 billion parameters, so they can also watch how the phenomenon changes with scale.

**[Slide 5 — Datasets]**
The foundation of the paper is dataset design. Rather than scrape messy claims from the web, they built simple, templated, unambiguous datasets where truth is essentially the only thing that varies. There's a cities dataset — "The city of Paris is in France" — number comparisons like "sixty-one is larger than seventy-four," Spanish-to-English translation statements, and conjunctions and disjunctions built from the city statements. Crucially, they also built negated versions of key datasets — the same statements with "not" inserted — because negation is exactly where earlier probes fell apart. They kept a few messier datasets from prior work as stress tests.

**[Slide 6 — The likely control]**
One confound deserves its own slide: plausibility. True statements usually sound more probable than false ones, so maybe a "truth probe" is really a "probable text probe." To rule that out, they built a control dataset called "likely" — nonfactual text where the final token is either the most likely completion or the hundredth most likely one. And here's the key: in the negated datasets, truth and probability actually anti-correlate — after "The city of Paris is not in," the model still finds "France" the most probable continuation, even though it makes the statement false. So the datasets are arranged so that truth and plausibility can be pulled apart.

**[Slide 7 — Localization]**
Before probing, they had to decide where to look. A transformer produces a hidden state at every token in every layer. To find out, they used activation patching. They take one hidden state from a run where the statement is true, transplant it into a run where it's false, and measure how much the model's true-versus-false judgment moves. Doing this one position and one layer at a time turned up three groups of important states: one over the city name, one late in the network that basically holds the model's answer, and a third group that matters most for this talk. That third group sits over the final token of the statement and its end-of-sentence punctuation. These states seem to summarize the whole statement, and they influence the model's verdict. For the 13B model, that's the layer-15 residual stream over the punctuation token. Everything from here on studies those summary states.

**[Slide 8 — PCA]**
Now the picture that gives the paper its title. Take those summary-state activations, run PCA — plain, textbook principal component analysis — and plot the top two components. In the 13B and 70B models, true statements fall on one side and false statements on the other, separated along a line. No classifier was trained to make this picture; the linear structure is just there in the geometry. The 7-billion-parameter model looks different. Its clusters are organized by surface features — you can find a blob of statements containing the word "eighty" — which is about spelling, not truth. In the bigger models, this line shows up by the early-middle layers, and a bit later for more complex statements like conjunctions.

**[Slide 9 — Alignment and scale]**
The alignment story is more subtle, and I think it's the most interesting part of the paper. Sometimes the separation directions for different datasets line up, and sometimes they don't. In the 13-billion-parameter model, larger-than and smaller-than separate along opposite directions. And in its middle layers, the direction that separates true from false city statements is almost at a right angle to the direction for their negations. The authors' interpretation is that mid-scale models represent a simpler concept — something like "close association." Paris is associated with France; that correlates with truth for plain statements and anti-correlates once you negate them. In the 70-billion-parameter model, the two number datasets line up along one shared direction. You can even watch a similar shift inside the 13B model itself. Follow cities and their negations layer by layer, and their directions go from opposite, to orthogonal, to aligned. The picture is hierarchical — surface features first, then association, then, at scale, something like abstract truth.

**[Slide 10 — Probing methods]**
Next, the probing comparison. To test generalization, they train each probe on 80 percent of one dataset, then measure its accuracy on every other dataset. Three methods: logistic regression, the standard; CCS, an unsupervised method built from contrast pairs; and the paper's own proposal, mass-mean probing — which is simply the vector from the average false activation to the average true activation, with an optional covariance correction that's essentially linear discriminant analysis from 1936. Why prefer the simple method? Because logistic regression is trained to separate classes, and if some other feature sits at an angle to the truth direction, regression will happily tilt its boundary to exploit it. You get a great classifier pointing in the wrong direction. The difference in means can't play that trick — it points from the center of one cluster to the center of the other.

**[Slide 11 — Generalization results]**
And the simple method holds up. For the 13B and 70B models, probes generalize across datasets well, and mass-mean probing matches the fancier techniques. And a plain few-shot prompting baseline, where you just ask the model true or false, turns out to be weak by comparison. My favorite number in the paper: a probe trained only on number comparisons — larger-than plus smaller-than — scores over 95 percent accuracy on the Spanish translation dataset it never saw. Training on statements together with their opposites improves transfer, especially to other negated datasets — fixing exactly the failure mode that embarrassed the earlier probes. Meanwhile, probes trained on the "likely" control do fine where probability and truth agree, and fall below chance where they disagree — confirming the direction tracks truth, not plausibility.

**[Slide 12 — Causal interventions]**
But classification accuracy can't prove the model itself uses this direction. So the final experiment intervenes: while the model processes statements from a dataset the probe never trained on, they add or subtract the probe direction at those summary states, and check whether the model's verdict flips. Effects are measured with normalized indirect effects — zero means nothing happened, one means a flipped statement is judged with the same confidence as a genuine one. Mass-mean directions win seven of the eight conditions, often by a wide margin. On the 13B model, directions learned from cities and their negations score 0.85 and 0.97 for the mass-mean probe, against 0.33 and 0.52 for logistic regression — despite the two probes having nearly identical accuracy. That's the methodological punchline of the paper: a probe that predicts is not the same as a probe that explains.

**[Slide 13 — Limitations]**
Now the fine print, which the authors state plainly. The statements are simple and uncontroversial by design, so the experiments can't fully separate "true" from close cousins like "commonly believed" or "verifiable." Only one model family was studied. And a couple of results remain unexplained — for instance, directions from the "likely" dataset intervene oddly well even though they classify poorly. One caveat of my own: every intervention number is measured on a single test set, the Spanish translation statements, so I'd want to see it replicated elsewhere. So this is not a lie detector. It's a controlled result about representation geometry.

**[Slide 14 — Takeaways]**
To wrap up: at sufficient scale, large language models linearly represent the truth of factual statements — you can see it with PCA, read it off with probes that transfer across topics, and steer the model's judgments by pushing along the direction. The simplest probe, a difference in means, turns out to identify the most causal direction. And for me, the broader lesson is about craft: controlled dataset design, an explicit confound control, and a causal validation step did more for this result than any classifier sophistication could. The idea that we might one day audit what a model says by examining what it represents is still early — but this paper shows the needle exists. Thanks for watching.
